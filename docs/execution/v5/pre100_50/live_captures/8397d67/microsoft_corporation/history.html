2009 — as the record stood in July 2010

What was true thenObserved

By July 2010 the record showed 3 published financial year(s), the most recent 2009 at an index of 105, earned at a 38.6% operating margin — growing 9.9% a year. That is what a decision at this date had to work from.

What management didObserved

The choice is legible in the figures it filed: revenue growth accelerated and the margin widened with it — growth the cost base was absorbing. For this kind of business the levers available were pricing and packaging, sales motion and coverage and retention and expansion programmes.

What the market expectedModelled

On the record as it stood in July 2010, continuing at 2.3% and reverting toward 15% would have put the index near 661 by 2025. This is modelled from information published by that date — it is not a retrieved consensus and no analyst said it.

What happenedObserved

By 2025 the index reached 589 — 11% behind what the record at the time implied. This card is the only one on this page built from material filed after July 2010.

Better alternativeCounterfactual

Press the advantage: fund the next expansion while the cost base is still absorbing the current one. The same revenue path at a higher margin, compounding.

Why it might have workedCounterfactual

The cost base of a subscription business is largely fixed, so holding hiring flat while contracted revenue keeps compounding turns the same top line into a materially higher margin — and the index here counts the margin the growth was earned at.

What could have invalidated itCounterfactual

An expansion funded on the strength of one good year is a bet that the year was structural, and a cycle turning underneath it is exactly what makes it look structural. The assumption it rests on is that the cost base absorbing this year's growth will absorb next year's, which holds while the margin is widening and stops the moment it is not — and that was checkable at the time, which is what makes this an argument rather than a preference.
